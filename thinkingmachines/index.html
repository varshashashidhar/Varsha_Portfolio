Case study · Sourcing data in a new domain

Pilot before you scale

Research priorities change fast, and many data requests arrive in domains nobody on the team has sourced before. When the first big batch is the first real test of a vendor, quality problems show up after the budget and the timeline are already spent.

Problem

Open-ended asks go straight to whichever vendor the team knows best. Quality is judged on the full delivery, and what the team learned about a vendor or a domain lives in someone's head and a Slack thread, so the next new domain starts from zero.

Bet

Turn every request into a one-page brief with a small gold set, run the same paid pilot with two or three vendors, review the pilots side by side with the researcher, then scale with a split plan and keep a scorecard that carries over to the next domain.

How we'd know

More deliveries accepted on the first pass, faster time from request to a usable first batch, and lower cost per accepted item, while researchers spend less time explaining what they meant.

01

Context

Thinking Machines is training frontier models and building Tinker so people can make models their own. The Data team helps researchers get the data they need for training and evaluation: understanding the request, finding the right vendors, experts, or sources, reviewing quality with researchers, and delivering on research timelines. The work changes with research priorities, and much of it involves vendors in other countries.

02

Problem & users

Take a request like this: "We need a lot of hard chemistry reasoning data, ideally expert-written, for the next post-training run in about four weeks." It's a real need, but it isn't a spec yet. How hard is hard? How much is a lot? Does textbook-style content count? Each unanswered question becomes a guess that a vendor makes for us, and we discover the guesses when the first 1,000 items arrive.

Researcher

"I know good data when I see it. I just don't have time to write a 10-page spec."

Pain: finds out a dataset is off when training results look wrong.

Data operations

"Three vendors say they can do chemistry. I can't tell which one actually can until I've paid for a full batch."

Pain: picking vendors on reputation, not evidence.

Vendor team lead

"Give us examples of what good looks like, and tell us early when we're off."

Pain: vague briefs, and feedback that arrives after the work is done.

03

Why now

  • The valuable data is getting more specialized. Post-training and evaluation increasingly need expert judgment in narrow domains, where a generalist vendor's best guess isn't good enough.
  • Priorities shift quickly. A small team can't afford a sourcing process that restarts from zero every time research moves into a new area.
  • More vendors, more countries. Comparing vendors fairly, and handling contracts and compliance across borders, needs a light, repeatable structure.

05

Metrics

Hypotheses I'd validate with real baselines before setting targets.

North star

First-pass acceptance rate

Share of delivered items researchers accept without a redo. If pilots pick the right vendors and the brief is clear, this rises.

Input

Request → first usable batch

Days from a researcher's first message to a batch they'd train on.

Input

Pilot-to-scale prediction

How closely pilot agreement predicts quality at full volume. Tells us whether 50 items is enough.

Outcome

Cost per accepted item

Total spend divided by accepted items, including pilots and redo work.

Guardrail

Researcher time and speed

Researcher hours per project and total time to delivery. The process fails if pilots add more delay than they save.

06

Rollout

  1. Phase 1 · One project

    Brief, gold set, and pilot on the next new-domain request. Run it by hand in a spreadsheet and measure time, cost, and first-pass acceptance against the last comparable project.

  2. Phase 2 · Make it repeatable

    Templates and a vendor scorecard. Turn the brief and pilot review into templates, and keep a shared scorecard of every vendor's pilot results by domain, cost, speed, and contract status.

  3. Phase 3 · Automate the routine

    Light tooling where judgment isn't needed. Auto-score pilots against the gold set, check for textbook overlap, and draft vendor feedback by issue type, while people keep reviewing the samples.

07

Risks & open questions

Tradeoffs I'd watch

  • Pilots slow urgent work. Skip or shrink the pilot for domains where a vendor already has a strong scorecard.
  • A small gold set misleads. Track how well pilot results predict full-batch quality, and grow the gold set when they don't.
  • Vendors tune to the gold set. Keep gold items hidden and refresh them between projects.
  • Cross-border contracts. Work with legal early so contract status never blocks a vendor that won the pilot.

Questions I'd bring to the team

  • Which recent projects needed the most redo work, and why?
  • How do researchers prefer to define "good" today: examples, rubrics, or review sessions?
  • What's the right pilot size for pre-training data versus post-training or evals?
  • Which data types could come from unusual sources instead of vendors at all?
